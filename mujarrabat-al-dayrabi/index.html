Then fighting erupted in the mosque and his sight started to go. He went back to the work and saw it through, and Cairo has never let it fall out of print. What he left behind

Thirty-six chapters and a conclusion. In them al-Dayrabī records the properties he attributes to Qur'anic verses and the Divine Names, alongside prayers, charms, amulets, magic squares and cures: the Fātiḥa and the Verse of the Throne, safeguarding travellers, the evil eye, fever, childbirth and, at the end, oddities ranging from holding fire to drawing stains out of cloth.

In the evil-eye chapter he sets down a tale that an earlier scholar had recorded: a man in Khurāsān whose glance, it was claimed, could bring down a camel. That chapter forms the free sample, allowing the book to be weighed up before purchase.

Threaded through the margins of the Cairo lithograph is a second work, the Mujarrabāt of Shaykh al-Sanūsī, also translated here in its entirety. Al-Dayrabī gave his own book the title Fatḥ al-Malik al-Majīd, The Opening of the Glorious King. He died in 1151 AH (1738 or 1739 CE).

How the pages appear

The author and the book

Aḥmad ibn ʿUmar al-Dayrabī, a Shāfiʿī jurist based at al-Azhar in Cairo, died in 1151 AH (1738 or 1739 CE). His Mujarrabāt, whose full title is Fatḥ al-Malik al-Majīd, has been reprinted in Cairo without interruption ever since. As far as we have been able to determine, no English edition has previously appeared. According to his own account, work on the text halted in 1123 AH, after sword fighting erupted within al-Azhar.

This is a historical document, and it is offered as such. Al-Dayrabī wrote in the voice of a believer and a legal scholar, addressing readers who inhabited the same world as he did. The customs he describes are rendered as he set them down, with neither dilution nor approval: they are the practices of eighteenth-century Cairo and appear here as history, not as instruction.

How the translation came about

The rendering, produced by Anwar al-Ghundas, came from a Cairo lithograph and was checked against three further copies, one of which is an eighteenth-century manuscript kept at Exeter. Each numeral was read twice, separately, directly from the page. Any page that bears a figure appears in facsimile, and wherever the print is defective a brief note records the fact rather than making a guess.
