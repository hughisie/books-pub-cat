What this book is

Around the year 1200, a scholar from Annaba, in what is now Algeria, recorded a tradition that had been handed down orally from teacher to student. He catalogued the twenty-eight letters of the Arabic alphabet, assigning to each its numerical value, element and planetary association. He also set out the ninety-nine Names of God, together with the attributes that custom linked to each one. In addition, he described how to construct the balanced tables known as awfāq, and explained the purposes they were believed to serve.

Very few books have journeyed so widely. Its pages turned up in the libraries of the Ottoman court, in Sufi lodges throughout West Africa, among the Mughal rulers of India and within the brotherhoods of the Maghrib. For 800 years it was cited, debated and reproduced by scribes.

The publication has also drawn criticism. Al-Azhar in Cairo has repeatedly described it as a guide to siḥr, meaning prohibited sorcery. It appears on censorship lists in a number of nations. That background is genuine and contributes to the text's importance, yet it is not the reason for our decision to release it. We chose to publish it because a work with such influence ought to be fully accessible to any reader wishing to examine it.

What is in the 1,443 pages

Part One, the science of letters. Every one of the twenty-eight letters of the Arabic alphabet, with its numerical value, its element and its associated planet, together with al-Būnī's full reasoning presented in its entirety rather than condensed.

Part Two: the squares. The awfāq, starting with the three-by-three grid and larger, along with the methods for constructing them. In this issue, each one is a clear diagram that readers can trace and reproduce.

Part Three: The Names of God. This section covers the ninety-nine Names, along with their numerical values and the characteristics that tradition attributes to each one.

Part Four, the practical chapters. The rituals and operations that gave the volume its standing, rendered in complete and unabridged translation, with commentary where the manuscripts differ. Four briefer works published after the Fourth Part are likewise included.

Parts One and Two appear in Volume One, while Volume Two contains Parts Three and Four, along with the four additional treatises appended to the work. An introductory preface sets out the editorial method, and a separate note specifies the edition used for the print text.

On the phrase "first complete translation". Several English selections from this work exist, and some are good. One is recommended on our comparison page. What did not exist until now is the whole text of all four parts in English. That is the exact claim, and the only one we make.

How it was made, in five facts

  1. The source text is taken from the Cairo edition of 1928 (1347 AH), produced by the Ḥusayniyya Egyptian Press, as found in the 546-page digitised version held by the American University of Beirut.
  2. A large language model generated the draft by processing each page image individually, and each entry in every table was transcribed directly from the visual rather than taken from any prior translation.
  3. The publisher verified each section the model had identified as uncertain by comparing it with the image.
  4. Eighty-nine uncertain readings are still flagged in the document.

The full statement, together with the areas that remain uncertain, can be found on the about page.

Why a second edition was needed. The original version relied on optical character recognition of a scanned document. As a result, it lacked the illustrations, and one critic pointed this out. That criticism was fair. For this release, the text has been retranslated directly from the page images, and all 483 figures are now included.

Honest answers to the awkward questions

Are the magic squares actually in it?

Indeed, presented as figures. We state this without ambiguity, because the initial edition omitted them and a critic flagged the absence. That criticism was valid. The squares are central to the book, and an edition that merely discusses them fails to be comprehensive. They are included here, all 483, each rendered both as a transcription and as a plate of the original printed page.

How was the translation made?

The summary above covers the essentials. For the complete story, including the role of automation and the extent of human review, see the about page. We prefer to be upfront before you subscribe, rather than leave you to discover the details later.

Will it work?

This is a rendering of a historical text. We offer no assurance, whether explicit or implicit, that any method described within it yields a material outcome. How you choose to use this material is your own affair, and we guarantee nothing, for there is nothing to guarantee.

Is it legal where I live?

For the most part, yes. However, in certain nations the book is subject to restrictions or an outright ban, and border officials may confiscate a physical copy. We are unable to offer guidance on the legal position in your location, and we will not dispatch the printed version to territories where it is known to be forbidden. The electronic format, by contrast, avoids any customs concerns.

Does this version surpass the other English editions?

This is the sole complete edition available, and that is the full extent of our assertion. For scholarly purposes, another version may serve you better, and we identify it on our editions comparison. Ensuring you obtain the correct publication is our priority over making a sale.

Do I need to read Arabic?

No. Technical terminology is transliterated and clarified on its first mention. Where an English rendering would strip a term of its intended sense, the transliteration is retained with an accompanying explanation, rather than being simplified.
