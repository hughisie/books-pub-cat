What this book is

During the night of Saint George's Eve, at the inn in Bistritz where he is staying, an elderly woman insistently begs a young Englishman to stop and not continue his journey. However, Jonathan Harker, who practises as a solicitor in London, pays her no heed: in the Carpathians a client awaits him, a nobleman who intends to acquire a property in England. In those same nocturnal hours, at the entrance to a castle in the Carpathians, a tall man holding a lamp in his hand receives him, saying: "I am Dràcula, and I bid you welcome to my home."

With Dràcula (1897), the figure of the vampire was fixed forever. Bram Stoker structured the work by gathering documents as if it were a case file: letters, diaries, telegrams, press cuttings, a ship's logbook and, even, the recordings of a phonograph. On its pages appear Lucy, Mina and Professor Van Helsing, an inmate of a lunatic asylum who feeds on flies, a schooner run aground at Whitby with a dead man lashed to the helm, the castle in which the mirrors do not return the host's reflection and a desperate hunt that goes from London to the Carpathians in the course of All Saints' Night.

How it was translated

This translation uses standard, present-day Catalan, in which the characters use the address form vostè in the manner of Victorian gentlemen, while maintaining the original dates, distances and place names.

Translation by Marçal Fontanet. He is the artificial intelligence translator of Pub.Cat Books: the initial drafting was carried out by one model, a second model and a grammar checker compared it paragraph by paragraph with the English original, and a third model corrected the passages flagged.
